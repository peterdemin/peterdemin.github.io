6. Interactive example: Stretchy flag on the Moon#

Before adding air, let us try the mechanics in Elastic cloth model. This experiment has a soft, stretchy flag, a fixed vertical support, and gravity. There is no wind, no air drag, and no horizontal rod supporting the top edge.

The fixed left edge cannot move. The rest of the cloth is free to move unless our temporary hand holds its top-right corner. Reset puts that hand back so the flag is ready to grab. Releasing it removes the hand’s support; it does not turn off gravity or detach the left edge.

We use lunar gravitational acceleration of 1.62 m/s², from NASA’s Moon Fact Sheet. This means gravity changes downward velocity by 1.62 m/s each second when no other force opposes it. It is weaker than Earth’s gravity, not zero.

6.1. Try the flag#

Grab the glowing corner, pull it away from the support, and let go. Pull up or down as well as sideways. The camera stays in front; the lighting changes across the surface as the cloth bends, not because the camera moves. Use Reset flag to begin again, or Let go to release the held corner. Touch works too. For keyboard control, focus the corner: Space grabs or releases it, arrow keys pull, and Escape lets go.

If the embedded view does not load, open the flag experiment on its own. JavaScript and WebGL, the browser’s three-dimensional graphics system, are required.

6.2. What to look for#

Stretch. Pull outward and watch the small grid squares lengthen. The cloth is deliberately much more compliant, meaning easier to deform, than the sail demonstration’s nearly unstretchable links. Which part stretches most: near your hand, near the support, or across the middle?

Shear. Pull the corner upward or downward. Squares can become slanted four-sided shapes: their thread directions change angle. That is the shear introduced in the cloth chapter, not just a change in their edge lengths.

Bending. Watch the smooth changes of surface direction and the highlights across them. Bending can change the shape without requiring the same amount of extension everywhere. Here neighboring patches resist abruptly different turns, so the flag drapes rather than forming a prescribed sharp crease.

Gravity and support. Release the corner after a substantial pull. The free cloth falls, swings, and drapes downward while the left edge stays fixed. Immediately after release, stored elastic energy can first pull it back or even briefly upward. Gravity still acts throughout that recoil. The final flag is not in free fall: the support continues to carry its weight.

6.3. A soft material and an intentional limit#

The cloth chapter now includes the finite-strain energy used here. Each triangle measures the lengths and angle of its material directions, rather than substituting a diagonal spring for shear. Its energy includes both directional extension, their coupling, and shear, multiplied by the triangle’s original area. The choices are extension stiffness 35 N/m, coupling stiffness 3.5 N/m, and shear stiffness 0.35 N/m. These have the same material meanings as the chapter’s coefficients, with much softer extension. They are illustrative, not measured flag properties.

Bending uses the chapter’s discrete hinge energy: adjacent triangle normals turn through an angle, weighted using original edge lengths and triangle areas. Its chosen discrete rigidity is 0.2 N m. Flat uneven stretching no longer creates false bending energy. The hinge law still has a directional bias from the triangle grid; it is not a complete isotropic shell model.

The displayed local stretch is the largest fractional length increase along either material direction in any triangle. A separate limiting rule stops these lengths at approximately 1.55 times their original values, or 55% extension. It does not bound every possible diagonal direction. Once the limit is reached, pulling farther cannot keep extending it, although the cloth can still change direction and shape.

Both the finite-strain material law and this inequality are now defined in the cloth chapter. The inequality is an intentional teaching addition, not an inherent maximum of the energy law. Neither the finite-strain extension nor the cap is a measured prediction for real fabric at 55% stretch. Real cloth may stiffen, lose energy, or tear instead.

6.4. What this experiment leaves out#

The model uses small artificial damping, reducing velocity at a chosen rate of 0.35 per second, to remove energy and make settling usable. This is not air resistance in a lunar vacuum, and the settling time is not a measured prediction for real cloth.

Tearing and contact with the ground or support are not calculated. Cloth touching itself is not calculated either, so folds may overlap or pass through each other. We deliberately leave that contact problem out of this experiment.

6.5. The numerical update#

The computer retains each vertex’s position and velocity, its mass from reference triangle area, and the triangle/hinge connections. Its ordinary physical tick is 1/120 s. Unlike the free satellite’s RK4 integration, this update uses extended position-based dynamics, XPBD: it predicts positions, then adjusts them with compliant constraints derived from the cloth energies. Compliant means allowing deformation according to energy cost instead of demanding perfectly rigid geometry. Algorithm 1 in the XPBD paper describes this prediction/correction pattern.

For one free vertex’s vertical component, let \(y\) be starting height in metres, \(v\) starting velocity in m/s, \(a=-1.62\) m/s², and \(h=1/120\) s the tick duration. Let \(\beta=0.35\) per second be the artificial damping rate. Subscript pred labels a temporary prediction. The exponential \(\exp\) supplies a positive reduction factor from the dimensionless product \(-\beta h\):

\[v_{\mathrm{pred}}=\exp(-\beta h)(v+a h), \qquad y_{\mathrm{pred}}=y+h v_{\mathrm{pred}}.\]

Gravity first changes velocity, then damping reduces all velocity components by the same factor, about 0.99709 per tick. This is neither lunar air resistance nor calibrated material loss.

One sweep visits all of the tick’s constraints without advancing time again. The solver makes 12 sweeps of membrane-energy and hinge-energy corrections plus directional extension limits. It can add up to 60 limit-only sweeps when needed. These are the triangle-strain and geometric hinge laws in the cloth chapter, not independent diagonal springs. Pins and the held corner have prescribed positions. Correction bookkeeping starts at zero for each tick and accumulates only while solving that tick.

Let \(y_{\mathrm{new}}\) be accepted corrected height and \(v_{\mathrm{new}}\) its reconstructed velocity. The code uses

\[v_{\mathrm{new}}=\frac{y_{\mathrm{new}}-y}{h}.\]

The same rule applies to all three components. Elastic effects already included in the position correction are not added again as explicit forces. If finite-value or extension checks fail, the code restores old positions, halves old velocities, and does not advance physical time. The display still consumes that attempted tick’s budget. Rendering is separate from physics; capped work can make slow devices lag wall-clock time. Its paths are calculated from the current cloth state, gravity, and your hand, not drawn from a preselected falling animation. This isolates the cloth questions; Airflow model introduces the air that the sail must interact with. The solver makes a finite number of energy-derived corrections each step, not an exact minimization or an energy-conserving calculation. Slow devices may advance simulated time more slowly than wall-clock time.
